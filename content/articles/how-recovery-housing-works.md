Recovery housing is a place to live, not a treatment level. A recovery residence is alcohol-free and illicit-drug-free housing where residents support each other, and in Virginia it offers no clinical treatment by definition. Clients combine it with treatment, often outpatient, that they get from a separate provider. The two connect through the client and through you, so you are the one who checks the house.

**In brief**

- Virginia law says a recovery residence "does not include clinical treatment services" ([Va. Code § 37.2-431.1(A)](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/37.2-431.1/)).
- The National Alliance for Recovery Residences (NARR) sorts housing into four levels of support, from peer-run Oxford Houses to clinical programs.
- Virginia requires certification to operate. Certification is not a clinical license (our reading), and the state does not currently inspect (as of the latest report).
- Before you refer, check who owns the house, whether the stay depends on one program, and what the written agreement says. The questions are at the end.

A client finishes a residential stay, or starts intensive outpatient, and needs somewhere stable to live that is not the place they used. The rest of this article explains how recovery housing works, how it sits next to treatment, and how to check a house.

## What a recovery residence is

SAMHSA, the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, calls recovery housing "a recovery support service" in which "the recovery housing setting is the service" ([SAMHSA, Best Practices for Recovery Housing, 2023, PEP23-10-00-002, copy hosted by the West Virginia Bureau for Behavioral Health](https://bbh.wv.gov/media/35510/download), Overview).

The residents supply much of the support: shared rules, accountability to each other, and a household that stays sober. The federal law that required SAMHSA's guidance also told it to leave out best practices for treatment services ([SAMHSA, Background](https://bbh.wv.gov/media/35510/download)).

Virginia puts the line in statute. A recovery residence is alcohol-free and illicit-drug-free housing for people with substance use disorders (SUDs), with or without a co-occurring mental illness, "that does not include clinical treatment services" ([Va. Code § 37.2-431.1(A)](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/37.2-431.1/)).

Under that definition a recovery residence is not a treatment level. It delivers no clinical treatment, and a client living in one still needs treatment from a separate provider. NARR also defines a Level IV that includes clinical services, covered below. For what the treatment levels are, read [levels of care explained](/articles/levels-of-care-explained/).

## NARR levels I to IV

NARR sorts housing into four levels of support. It treats the terms level and type as interchangeable, and its levels are informed by the categories of the American Society of Addiction Medicine (ASAM) ([NARR standards](https://narronline.org/standards/)).

- **Level I, peer-run.** "Democratically run" homes, with Oxford Houses as an example. SAMHSA's table lists no on-site paid staff ([SAMHSA, Table 1](https://bbh.wv.gov/media/35510/download)).
- **Level II, monitored.** Often called sober homes or sober living, with house rules, peer accountability and a house manager.
- **Level III, supervised.** Adds weekly, structured programming such as peer recovery support and life skills, with staff who are "supervised, trained, or credentialed."
- **Level IV, clinical.** NARR says Level IV "integrates the social and medical model," and programs lacking social model principles do not qualify.

Level IV is where Virginia diverges. A state workgroup report says Level 4 residences "offer clinical treatment services and are required to be licensed by DBHDS," the Virginia Department of Behavioral Health and Developmental Services ([Senate Document 11, 2025](https://rga.lis.virginia.gov/Published/2025/SD11/PDF)). The DBHDS page describes houses of the Virginia Association of Recovery Residences (VARR) described as "Level Four Houses," and service providers with in-house clinical services ([DBHDS](https://dbhds.virginia.gov/office-of-recovery-services/recovery-residences)).

Because the statute's definition excludes clinical services, a Level IV program is a licensed provider rather than a certified recovery residence (our reading). The certification rules below cover Levels I to III.

## Oxford Houses

NARR names Oxford Houses as an example of Level I, and they work differently from a sober living home with a manager. In Virginia, Oxford House, Inc. is its own credentialing entity.

Oxford Houses are run by their members. There is no house manager or outside authority, members elect officers to six-month terms, every member pays an equal share of expenses, and there is no time limit on how long a member can live there. A member who returns to using alcohol or drugs "must be immediately expelled," and the house votes on who joins ([Oxford House](https://www.oxfordhouse.org/)).

The traditions describe each house as self-run and self-supporting, and say it "should remain forever non-professional" ([Oxford House traditions](https://www.oxfordhouse.org/house-traditions)).

Virginia requires an Oxford House to tell prospective residents it is "self-governed and unstaffed" ([Va. Code § 37.2-431.1(B)](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/37.2-431.1/)). You can search houses and vacancies by state on [Oxford House's vacancy site](https://www.oxfordvacancies.com/).

## How residents combine housing with treatment

Many residents stay in recovery housing during or after outpatient treatment. SAMHSA says housing providers should "offer resources to help residents access and remain in outpatient treatment." At Levels I and II the housing relies on outside clinical services "as needed" or "available and encouraged." At Level III it is "linked with" clinical services in the community ([SAMHSA, Best Practice 10 and Table 1](https://bbh.wv.gov/media/35510/download)).

In practice that means two arrangements running side by side: a house with its own rules and rent, and a treatment program, such as intensive outpatient, with its own schedule and clinical record. Which housing fits which client is a clinical judgment for you and the treatment team, and this article does not make it.

Payment follows the split. VARR, which accredits Virginia residences to NARR standards, puts it this way: "Recovery residences are self pay and are NOT COVERED BY INSURANCE." It points to the statewide Indigent Bed Program for people who cannot pay ([VARR directory](https://varronline.org/accredited-residences-2/)).

## What Virginia certification requires

Virginia treats certification as the entry ticket. Nobody may operate a recovery residence, or advertise or imply state certification, without certification from DBHDS. A violation is a Class 1 misdemeanor (subsection C of [Virginia's recovery-residence law](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/37.2-431.1/)). The statute lets the regulations require accreditation or membership, and the regulation does. A certified residence must:

- hold a credential, accreditation or charter from VARR or Oxford House, Inc. ([12VAC35-260-20](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/admincodefull/title12/agency35/chapter260/)). DBHDS identifies those two as the credentialing entities ([DBHDS](https://dbhds.virginia.gov/office-of-recovery-services/recovery-residences)).
- tell prospective residents its credentialing entity and its NARR level, or its Oxford House status (statute, subsection B).
- meet square-footage requirements and report any death or serious injury on its premises (subsection C).
- not require residents to use medical or psychological services, including clinical SUD treatment, from which it financially benefits, directly or indirectly (subsection C).

DBHDS must publish lists of certified and conditionally certified residences (subsection D). Conditional certification exists for operators still working toward accreditation, and it is not granted, according to DBHDS, to anyone who has not begun that process ([DBHDS](https://dbhds.virginia.gov/office-of-recovery-services/recovery-residences)).

State agencies may refer only to certified residences, and no court may refer or release a person to one that is not certified (subsection F).

## What certification does not do

Certification is a registration and credentialing requirement, not a clinical license or a promise of quality (our reading). Four limits are documented.

- **No treatment.** By definition the residence offers no clinical services ([§ 37.2-431.1(A)](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/37.2-431.1/)).
- **No state inspection (as of the latest report).** The 2025 workgroup report finds that DBHDS staff "do not currently inspect recovery residences," and found an overreliance on third-party credentialing agencies without adequate state support and oversight ([SD 11](https://rga.lis.virginia.gov/Published/2025/SD11/PDF)). More on this below.
- **No insurance coverage.** See VARR's statement above.
- **Data rules still coming.** A 2026 act orders regulations "regarding sharing of data related to recovery residences with the public." The shared data may include items such as certification status, inspection results and incident counts, effective January 1, 2027 ([2026 Acts, Chapter 1000, enactments 4 and 5](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/uncodifiedacts/2026/session1/chapter1000/)). VARR and DBHDS already publish lists, and VARR publishes inspection dates.

### Where inspection stands

Inspection may change, so check the current position with DBHDS.

- A 2026 act amends the charge of the same workgroup. The Department now convenes it, it reports annually by November 1, and it expires July 1, 2031. Local fire, building, safety and health inspection methods are among its topics ([2026 Acts, Chapter 1000, enactment 6](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/uncodifiedacts/2026/session1/chapter1000/)).
- A DBHDS workgroup draft of 12VAC35-260, dated August 25, 2026, is not adopted. In section 12VAC35-260-50 it lets the department conduct an inspection on application, and accredited or chartered residences "shall not be required to have an inspection." It also lets the department conduct unannounced inspections of all recovery residences at any time to determine compliance, and provides for inspections on complaints and serious incidents ([draft](https://dbhds.virginia.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Recovery-Residence-Workgroup-draft.pdf)).
- VARR does annual on-site inspections, according to the DBHDS page ([DBHDS](https://dbhds.virginia.gov/office-of-recovery-services/recovery-residences)).

## How a counselor finds and vets a house

Start with the lists. DBHDS links its certified list, its conditional list, the Oxford House vacancy site and VARR's directory from one page ([DBHDS](https://dbhds.virginia.gov/office-of-recovery-services/recovery-residences)). VARR's directory shows level, population served, location and last on-site inspection date. It warns that it "may not reflect all VARR accredited recovery residence operators" ([VARR](https://varronline.org/accredited-residences-2/)). Confirm the details with the house itself.

SAMHSA gives referrers a checklist ([SAMHSA, Referral Process](https://bbh.wv.gov/media/35510/download)). Look at:

- whether the home is "certified to national standards"
- house culture and level of support
- neighborhood and living environment
- current residents
- whether leadership supports medications for mental health and substance use disorders

SAMHSA also recommends that operators place no barriers or restrictions on prescribed medications, including buprenorphine, methadone and naltrexone ([SAMHSA, Best Practice 10](https://bbh.wv.gov/media/35510/download)).

Then ask for paper. For houses credentialed under NARR standards, NARR Standard 3.0 calls for a written agreement before a resident commits. It covers resident rights, financial obligations, services provided, recovery goals and relapse policies, plus a grievance process ([NARR Standard 3.0, Standards 5 and 7](https://narronline.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/NARR-Standard-3.0.pdf)). Oxford Houses follow Oxford House, Inc.'s own model rather than NARR's standard, so ask the house what its written terms are.

## What an undisclosed treatment tie can signal

A treatment program and a house can be connected. What SAMHSA identifies as dangerous is patient brokering: paying a third party to procure patients or residents. SAMHSA calls it "one of the more significant, life-threatening forms of healthcare/treatment fraud" ([SAMHSA, Best Practice 5](https://bbh.wv.gov/media/35510/download)).

Federal law covers this too. 18 U.S.C. § 220 covers knowingly and willfully soliciting, receiving, paying or offering remuneration for referring a patient or patronage to a recovery home, clinical treatment facility or laboratory, with respect to services covered by a health care benefit program. It also includes exceptions ([18 U.S.C. § 220](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/220)).

An undisclosed tie between a house and a program is a reason to ask more questions, not proof of wrongdoing. The statute's wording targets what a house requires of residents, not the connection itself.

**What Virginia law says now** ([§ 37.2-431.1](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/37.2-431.1/)):

- A residence must disclose its credentialing entity and level to residents. The text says nothing about disclosing a treatment relationship (subsection B).
- A residence may not require residents to use a service it profits from (subsection C).

**What may change:**

- The state workgroup recommended amending the Code to authorize the Board to require licensing of residences that require all residents to join the same outpatient program ([SD 11, Recommendation 2](https://rga.lis.virginia.gov/Published/2025/SD11/PDF)).
- A study of treatment providers that offer housing that is not licensed or certified was due by November 1, 2026 (not located; see below) ([2026 Acts, Chapter 1000, enactment 2](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/uncodifiedacts/2026/session1/chapter1000/)).

## Questions to ask before referring

Put these to any house or program:

- Who owns the house, and who owns the treatment program?
- Does staying here depend on attending one particular program?
- Does anyone pay or get paid for sending residents either way?
- Is the house on the DBHDS certified list under the credentialing entity it claims?
- Does the written agreement name every service and charge?

If the answers to the first three are unclear, we would take the question to your compliance officer before referring. SAMHSA says placement "should be predicated upon individual needs, goals, and choice" ([SAMHSA, Best Practice 2](https://bbh.wv.gov/media/35510/download)), so the choice stays your client's.

## Where this leaves you

Recovery housing sits next to treatment and works alongside it. Virginia certification tells you a house holds a credential and a state listing; it says little about how well the house is run (our reading). The checks above are yours to make, with the sources linked.
